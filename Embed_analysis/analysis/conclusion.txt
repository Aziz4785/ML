and here are my conclusions:
Users write output specifications, not requests. Each prompt is a bare noun phrase — no verb, no imperative, usually no article — whose head is a text-type (abstract, glossary entry, comment, biography) rather than a subject. Content is either absent or named only in broad strokes; what gets specified is form: length, register, domain, and style. The prompt describes the artifact to be produced, not an action to perform on a topic.
the shared template:
[LENGTH/DENSITY] [REGISTER] [DOMAIN] TEXT-TYPE
    ( + "in a ___ style/tone/register"
      | + "with ___"            (required content features)
      | + ", ___-ing clause"    (rhetorical action)
      | + "about / focused on ___" (broad topic) )*

Only the head noun is obligatory. Everything before it is a stack of adjectival modifiers; everything after it is an optional, repeatable post-modifier.

Also some queries are : 
- Attributive — what kind of text it is.
Stacked pre-modifiers (encyclopedic, journalistic, dictionary-style, informal, promotional, technical, formal, factual) plus in … style, with …, about …, focused on …, or participles (explaining, describing, summarizing). These groups specify register, genre, tone, domain, audience, and purpose.

Other are : Structural/sequential — how the text unfolds.
Relative clauses (text that begins…, text where…, passage which opens…) encoding an ordered discourse skeleton: opens with X, then Y, followed by Z, and ends with W. The slots are rhetorical moves — claim, qualification, correction, contrast, concession, reversal, example, call to action — or turn-taking events.
-----
some characteristics of many clusters: 
Abstraction over content. The slots are functional roles, not topics. "A claim, then a correction, then an example" — the subject matter is almost always left unspecified. Only a handful of groups pin a domain (mythology, science, promotional/commercial), and even there the domain is a modifier, not the point.
A consistent house dialect. Lowercase, article-less, telegraphic, comma-chained. Users have converged on something close to a domain-specific notation rather than natural request language.
